Stabilizer Codes and Channel Modeling: Characterizing Physical Noise in el-loom

This lesson focuses on how to rigorously and flexibly characterize quantum channels and physical noise on stabilizer codes within the el-loom framework from Entropica Labs. The target audience is PhD students and researchers already familiar with basic quantum information theory and quantum error correction (QEC).

1. From Physical Noise to Stabilizer Codes: Problem Setup

In experimental and theoretical research on quantum error correction, we often face the following levels of modeling problems:

  1. Physical layer: Single- and multi-qubit quantum channels \( \mathcal{N} \) describing noise and gate errors occurring in each physical time step.
  2. Code space layer: The “projection” or “effective action” of noise on stabilizer codes, such as logical channels, syndrome distributions, and error chain structures.
  3. Simulation layer: How to represent and propagate these channels in a numerically efficient way (in terms of bytes, matrices, Pauli spectra, etc.).

el-loom mainly provides:

  • Structured representations of quantum channels (Kraus form, Pauli channels, noise models).
  • Efficient simulation of stabilizer codes and Clifford circuits (expressed as Pauli frames / symplectic representations).
  • Application of error models on codes, error chain generation, and decoding interfaces.

This section starts from mathematical definitions and then gradually connects them to concrete APIs and usage in el-loom.

2. Mathematical Basics of Quantum Channels and Noise Models

2.1 Completely Positive Trace-Preserving Maps (CPTP maps)

A quantum channel on an \(n\)-qubit system is a linear map acting on density matrices \( \rho \in \mathcal{D}(\mathbb{C}^{2^n}) \): \[ \mathcal{N} : \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{2^n}) \to \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{2^n}) \] satisfying:

  • Completely positive: \[ (\mathcal{N} \otimes \mathbb{I}_k)(X) \succeq 0,\quad \forall X \succeq 0,\ \forall k \in \mathbb{N}. \]
  • Trace-preserving: \[ \mathrm{Tr}[\mathcal{N}(\rho)] = \mathrm{Tr}[\rho],\quad \forall \rho. \]

The most commonly used representation is the Kraus expansion: \[ \mathcal{N}(\rho) = \sum_{j} K_j \rho K_j^\dagger,\quad \sum_j K_j^\dagger K_j = I. \]

In el-loom, channels are usually represented in more structured forms (especially Pauli channels) in order to be compatible with the stabilizer framework.

2.2 Pauli Channels and Pauli Transformations

The Pauli group \( \mathcal{P}_n \) is \[ \mathcal{P}_n = \{ \omega P_1\otimes\cdots\otimes P_n \mid P_i\in\{I,X,Y,Z\},\ \omega\in\{\pm 1,\pm i\} \}. \] Ignoring global phases, we only care about \( n \)-qubit Pauli strings \[ P = P_1\otimes\cdots\otimes P_n,\quad P_i\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}. \]

A Pauli channel is a channel of the form \[ \mathcal{N}(\rho) = \sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_n/\{\pm 1,\pm i\}} p_P\,P \rho P^\dagger, \quad p_P \ge 0,\quad \sum_P p_P = 1. \] This includes many important models, such as:

  • Single-qubit depolarizing noise: \[ \mathcal{D}_p(\rho) = (1-p)\rho + \frac{p}{3}(X\rho X + Y\rho Y + Z\rho Z). \]
  • Independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) Pauli channels: \[ \mathcal{N}^{\otimes n} = \bigotimes_{i=1}^n \mathcal{N}_i,\quad \mathcal{N}_i(\rho) = \sum_{P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}} p_P P\rho P. \]

For stabilizer codes, Pauli channels are particularly important because the stabilizer formalism is closed and trackable under Pauli errors: Pauli errors remain Pauli errors after interacting with Clifford gates.

2.3 From General Channels to Pauli-Twirled Channels

In practice, physical noise is not necessarily of Pauli type, but in stabilizer-code analysis we often use the Pauli twirling approximation:

\[ \mathcal{N}_\text{twirl}(\rho) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_n|}\sum_{P\in \mathcal{P}_n} P^\dagger\,\mathcal{N}(P\rho P^\dagger)\,P. \]

One can show that \( \mathcal{N}_\text{twirl} \) is still CPTP and becomes diagonal in the Pauli basis, i.e., a Pauli channel. In el-loom, users can first perform this step analytically and then input the resulting Pauli channel into the simulation engine.

3. Mathematical Structure of Stabilizer Codes and Error Action

3.1 Stabilizer Group and Code Space

An \([[n,k,d]]\) stabilizer code is defined by an Abelian subgroup \[ \mathcal{S} \subset \mathcal{P}_n \] satisfying:

  • \(-I \notin \mathcal{S}\).
  • The joint +1 eigenspace of \(\mathcal{S}\) serves as the code space: \[ \mathcal{C} = \{ |\psi\rangle \in (\mathbb{C}^2)^{\otimes n} \mid S|\psi\rangle = |\psi\rangle,\ \forall S\in\mathcal{S} \}. \]
  • \(|\mathcal{S}| = 2^{n-k}\), hence \( \dim(\mathcal{C}) = 2^k \).

Given a set of independent stabilizer generators \( \{ S_1,\dots,S_{n-k} \} \), the action of any error \(E\in\mathcal{P}_n\) on the code space is determined by its commutation/anticommutation relations with the stabilizers.

For the syndrome vector \( s(E) \in \mathbb{F}_2^{n-k} \): \[ s_i(E) = \begin{cases} 0, & [E,S_i]=0,\\ 1, & \{E,S_i\}=0, \end{cases} \quad i=1,\dots,n-k. \]

3.2 Logical Operators and Distance

Logical Pauli operators \( \overline{X}_j, \overline{Z}_j \in \mathcal{P}_n \) satisfy:

  • \([\overline{X}_j, S_i] = 0\), \([\overline{Z}_j, S_i]=0,\ \forall i\).
  • \(\{\overline{X}_j,\overline{Z}_j\} = 0\), and for different \(j\neq l\) they mutually commute.
  • They correspond to nontrivial elements in \(\mathcal{P}_n/\mathcal{S}\).

The code distance \(d\) is defined as: \[ d = \min_{L\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{I\}} \mathrm{wt}(L), \] where \(\mathcal{L}\) is the logical Pauli group and \(\mathrm{wt}(\cdot)\) is the Hamming weight of a Pauli string.

3.3 Error Action and Syndrome Distribution

If the physical noise is a Pauli channel \[ \mathcal{N}(\rho) = \sum_{E\in\mathcal{P}_n} p_E\, E\rho E, \] and the initial state \( \rho \) lies in the code space \(\mathcal{C}\), then after measuring all stabilizer generators (or their corresponding syndrome qubits), the probability of obtaining syndrome \( s\in\mathbb{F}_2^{n-k} \) is \[ \Pr[s] = \sum_{E : s(E) = s} p_E. \] The unnormalized posterior state conditioned on syndrome \( s \) is \[ \rho_s' \propto \sum_{E : s(E)=s} p_E\, E\rho E. \]

If we only care about logical information, we can identify errors that differ by a stabilizer as belonging to the same “logical error class”: \[ E \sim E' \iff E'E^{-1} \in \mathcal{S}. \] For each syndrome \( s \), the posterior distribution over logical error classes is the input to the decoding algorithm; el-loom can generate these syndrome distributions and the corresponding error samples.

4.2 Single-Qubit Pauli Channels

Suppose we want to define a single-qubit Pauli channel \[ \mathcal{N}(\rho) = p_I \rho + p_X X\rho X + p_Y Y\rho Y + p_Z Z\rho Z, \quad p_I + p_X + p_Y + p_Z = 1. \] In el-loom this can be written as:

    "I": p_I,
    "X": p_X,
    "Y": p_Y,
    "Z": p_Z,

4.3 Standard Models such as Depolarizing Noise and Amplitude Damping

Many commonly used noise models typically have factory methods in el-loom, for example:

  • DepolarizingChannel: \[ \mathcal{D}_p(\rho) = (1-p)\rho + \frac{p}{3}(X\rho X+Y\rho Y+Z\rho Z). \]
  • AmplitudeDampingChannel: defined via Kraus operators \[ K_0 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0 & \sqrt{1-\gamma} \end{pmatrix}, \quad K_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \sqrt{\gamma}\\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \] so that \[ \mathcal{A}_\gamma(\rho) = K_0\rho K_0^\dagger + K_1\rho K_1^\dagger. \]

depol = DepolarizingChannel(p=p)

gamma = 0.005
amp_damp = AmplitudeDampingChannel(gamma=gamma)

As mentioned earlier, when integrating with stabilizer-code analysis, one typically needs to perform Pauli twirling or replace the model with an approximate Pauli channel. Some versions of el-loom provide tools to “Pauli-ify” a general channel (e.g., to_pauli_channel); please refer to the API documentation.

4.4 Custom Kraus Channels

For custom channels, you can directly specify the Kraus operators:

\[ \mathcal{N}(\rho) = \sum_j K_j \rho K_j^\dagger,\quad \sum_j K_j^\dagger K_j = I. \] 
import numpy as np
from loom.channels import KrausChannel

# Example: an arbitrary two-qubit channel
K0 = np.eye(4) * np.sqrt(0.9)
K1 = np.kron(np.array([[0, 1],[0, 0]]), np.eye(2)) * np.sqrt(0.1)

custom_kraus_channel = KrausChannel([K0, K1])

Note: To combine such general channels with stabilizer-code simulations, one usually needs additional processing (Pauli decomposition / twirling), otherwise large-scale simulation becomes difficult to perform efficiently.

5.3 Abstracting Encoding and Decoding Maps

A stabilizer code can be viewed as an isometry \[ V: \mathbb{C}^{2^k} \to \mathbb{C}^{2^n}, \] satisfying \[ V^\dagger S V = I,\ \forall S\in\mathcal{S}, \] and the implementation of logical operators on the code space is \[ \overline{X}_j = V X_j V^\dagger,\quad \overline{Z}_j = V Z_j V^\dagger. \]

In el-loom, one typically does not construct \(V\) explicitly, but instead encodes it implicitly via stabilizers and logical operators. For simulation purposes, the most crucial question is: How do we map a physical error \(E\) to its syndrome \(s(E)\) and logical error class \(\ell(E)\), rather than explicitly conjugating \(E\) by \(V\).

6. Action of Physical Noise on Stabilizer Codes: Mathematical Derivation

6.1 Action of the Encoding Channel

Consider the following process (a single error-correction cycle):

  1. Logical input state \( \rho_L \) (dimension \(2^k\)).
  2. Encoding isometry \(V\): \( \rho \mapsto V\rho_L V^\dagger \).
  3. Physical noise channel \( \mathcal{N} \): \( \rho \mapsto \mathcal{N}(\rho) \).
  4. Syndrome measurement and recovery map \( \mathcal{R} \).

Putting these together, we obtain the logical channel: \[ \mathcal{N}_L = \mathrm{Tr}_\mathrm{anc} \circ \mathcal{R} \circ \mathcal{N} \circ \mathcal{E}, \] where the encoding channel can be written in CPTP form as \[ \mathcal{E}(\rho_L) = V\rho_L V^\dagger. \]

If the noise is a Pauli channel \[ \mathcal{N}(\rho) = \sum_{E\in\mathcal{P}_n} p_E\, E\rho E, \] then on the encoded state we have: \[ \mathcal{N}(V\rho_L V^\dagger) = \sum_E p_E\, E V\rho_L V^\dagger E. \]

Measuring the syndrome is equivalent to grouping the errors \(E\) according to their syndrome \(s(E)\). Collecting all terms with the same syndrome gives \[ \mathcal{N}(V\rho_L V^\dagger) = \sum_s \sum_{E : s(E)=s} p_E\, E V\rho_L V^\dagger E. \]

6.2 Recovery Map and Logical Channel

Assume the recovery map \( \mathcal{R} \) consists of syndrome-dependent Clifford or Pauli corrections \(R_s\), i.e., \[ \mathcal{R}(\cdot) = \sum_s R_s \Pi_s (\cdot) \Pi_s R_s^\dagger, \] where \( \Pi_s \) is the projector onto the subspace corresponding to syndrome \(s\). Then for each syndrome \(s\):

\[ \rho_{L,s}' \propto V^\dagger R_s \Bigl( \sum_{E: s(E)=s} p_E\, E V\rho_LV^\dagger E \Bigr) R_s^\dagger V. \]

If \(R_s\) successfully maps most syndrome classes back into the code space, we can write a logical error operator as \[ R_s E V = V L_{s,E}, \] where \(L_{s,E}\in\mathcal{L}\) is some logical Pauli operator. Substituting this yields: \[ \rho_{L,s}' \propto \sum_{E : s(E)=s} p_E\, L_{s,E}\rho_L L_{s,E}. \]

Thus, for a given syndrome \(s\), the conditional logical channel is a Pauli channel \[ \mathcal{N}_{L|s}(\rho_L) = \sum_{L\in\mathcal{L}} q_{L|s}\, L\rho_L L, \] where \[ q_{L|s} = \frac{1}{\Pr[s]} \sum_{E: s(E)=s,\, L_{s,E}=L} p_E. \] If we ignore the syndrome, the overall logical channel is \[ \mathcal{N}_L(\rho_L) = \sum_s \Pr[s]\mathcal{N}_{L|s}(\rho_L) = \sum_L q_L\, L\rho_L L, \] with \[ q_L = \sum_s \Pr[s] q_{L|s} = \sum_E p_E \,\mathbf{1}[L_{s(E),E}=L]. \]

These logical error probabilities \(q_L\) and the syndrome distribution \( \Pr[s] \) can be estimated via numerical simulation—this is one of the key functionalities provided by el-loom.

7. Simulating Noise Action and Syndrome Generation in el-loom

7.1 Building a “Code + Noise + Measurement” Workflow

A typical el-loom workflow includes:

  1. Specify a stabilizer code code.
  2. Specify a physical noise model noise_model (usually a Pauli channel).
  3. Construct a circuit or process representing one “error correction cycle”.
  4. Use sampling to simulate many error instances and collect statistics on syndromes and logical errors.

Conceptual code (consult the official documentation for actual APIs): 

from loom.codes import SteaneCode
from loom.channels import PauliChannel
from loom.simulate import run_qec_cycle

code = SteaneCode()

# Define single-qubit Pauli noise
pI, pX, pY, pZ = 0.97, 0.01, 0.01, 0.01
single_qubit_pauli = PauliChannel.from_probs({
    "I": pI, "X": pX, "Y": pY, "Z": pZ,
})
noise_model = single_qubit_pauli.iid(num_qubits=code.n_qubits)

# Run many QEC cycles in simulation
num_shots = 100000
stats = run_qec_cycle(code=code, noise=noise_model, shots=num_shots)

print("Logical error rate:", stats.logical_error_rate)
print("Syndrome histogram:", stats.syndrome_counts)

Behind the scenes, a function like run_qec_cycle will:

  • In each shot, randomly sample an error \(E\) from the Pauli channel.
  • Compute its syndrome \(s(E)\) and the corresponding logical error class \(L_{s,E}\).
  • Accumulate frequencies of syndromes and logical error classes.

7.2 Pauli Error Sampling in el-loom

For a Pauli channel \[ \mathcal{N}(\rho) = \sum_{P} p_P P\rho P, \] if we are only interested in syndromes and logical error rates and do not need to explicitly track the state \(\rho\), we can use an “error sampling” strategy:

  1. Sample a Pauli error \(P\) according to probabilities \(p\) subscript \(P\), curly brace.-->\(\{p_P\}\).
  2. Compute \(s(P)\) and \(L(P)\).
  3. Treat \(P\) as the “actual error” without ever applying it to the state space.

This is the key source of efficiency in standard stabilizer-simulation frameworks. Internally, el-loom uses symplectic operations to compute \(s(P)\) and \(L(P)\) without matrix multiplications.

7.3 Multiple QEC Rounds and Time-Correlated Noise

To simulate multiple QEC rounds (e.g., \(T\) cycles):

\[ (\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{N})^T = \underbrace{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{N}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{N}}_{T\ \text{times}}. \]

In el-loom, you can:

  • Specify noise as the physical noise for each round (possibly time-dependent).
  • Use a loop to call run_qec_cycle repeatedly or use a dedicated multi-round API.
  • Accumulate logical error events (for example by measuring the logical qubit in the final round).

Representative code structure: 

T = 50   # number of QEC rounds
logical_error_events = 0

state = code.init_logical_state("0")  # abstract handle, not a full statevector
for t in range(T):
    result = run_qec_cycle(
        code=code,
        noise=noise_model,   # could be time-dependent: noise[t]
        state=state,
    )
    state = result.state   # logical state + Pauli frame
    if result.logical_error_occurred:
        logical_error_events += 1

logical_error_rate_per_cycle = logical_error_events / (num_shots * T)

In practice, state often consists of a “Pauli frame + syndrome history” rather than a full \(2^n\)-dimensional density matrix.

8. Variants of Channel Modeling: Gate Errors, Measurement Errors, and Spatio-Temporal Correlations

8.1 Noise Models on Clifford Gates

In experimental design, it is often more natural to model “a distinct noise channel for each gate”:

  • A single-qubit gate \(G\) is followed by a noise channel \( \mathcal{N}_G \).
  • Two-qubit gates (such as CNOT) have correlated error channels \(\mathcal{N}_\mathrm{CNOT}\).

In Clifford + Pauli-noise settings, a common modeling approach is:

\[ \mathcal{U}_G(\rho) = U_G \rho U_G^\dagger,\quad \mathcal{N}_G(\rho) = \sum_P p_{G,P} P\rho P, \] and the total channel is \[ \mathcal{E}_G = \mathcal{N}_G\circ\mathcal{U}_G. \]

el-loom usually provides an interface for describing “noisy gates” or “noisy circuits”, for example: 

from loom import circuits, noise

# Build a Clifford circuit for syndrome extraction
qc = circuits.StabilizerCircuit(code)

# Assign noise models to specific gate types
gate_noise = {
    "H": DepolarizingChannel(p=0.001),
    "CNOT": PauliChannel.from_probs({...}),
}

noisy_qc = noise.attach_gate_noise(qc, gate_noise)

8.2 Measurement Errors and Readout Noise

Measurement noise can be modeled as:

  • A classical confusion channel before z-basis measurement: \[ \Pr(\text{outcome} = b' \mid \text{true} = b) = M_{b'b}. \]
  • Equivalently: applying an \(X\) error before measurement with probability \(p_m\) (bit-flip readout error).

In syndrome measurements, measurement errors cause flips in syndrome bits. In el-loom, one can:

  • Directly apply classical bit-flip noise to measurement outcomes.
  • Or insert a Pauli \(X\) channel immediately before measurement.
meas_error_prob = 0.02

# Suppose we have a syndrome measurement circuit qc
noisy_meas = noise.add_measurement_bitflip(qc, p=meas_error_prob)

8.3 Spatially and Temporally Correlated Noise

In more realistic physical models, noise exhibits spatial correlations (e.g., nearby qubits undergoing correlated dephasing) and temporal correlations (e.g., \(1/f\) noise). Within the el-loom framework, this can be addressed via:

  1. Custom multi-qubit Pauli channels \( \mathcal{N} \) (e.g., two-body \(ZZ\) coherent errors).
  2. Defining different noise channels \( \mathcal{N}_t \) at each time step.
  3. Using Monte Carlo sampling of error histories \(\{E_t\}_t\) to study systematic behavior.

For example, a two-qubit correlated dephasing model: \[ \mathcal{N}(\rho) = (1-p)\rho + p Z_i Z_j \rho Z_i Z_j. \] 

from loom.channels import PauliChannel

# correlated ZZ error on qubits (i, j)
p = 0.001
correlated_zz = PauliChannel.from_probs({
    "I" * n: 1 - p,
    "I" * i + "Z" + "I" * (j - i - 1) + "Z" + "I" * (n - j - 1): p,
})

9. Example: Channel Modeling of Depolarizing Noise on the Steane Code

This section presents a concrete example of how to construct “Steane code + i.i.d. depolarizing noise” in el-loom, and how to compute the syndrome distribution and logical error rate.

9.1 Mathematical Setup

Consider the Steane \([[7,1,3]]\) code, and assume that each physical qubit undergoes a single-step depolarizing noise process in every QEC cycle: \[ \mathcal{D}_p(\rho) = (1-p)\rho + \frac{p}{3}\left(X\rho X + Y\rho Y + Z\rho Z\right). \] The total noise is \[ \mathcal{N} = \mathcal{D}_p^{\otimes 7}. \]

Since the code distance is \(d=3\), this code can correct all Pauli errors of weight \(\le 1\). For small \(p\), the dominant logical errors arise from weight-2 and weight-3 errors. The logical error rate scales roughly as \(O(p^2)\).

9.2 Implementation in el-loom

from loom.codes import SteaneCode
from loom.channels import DepolarizingChannel
from loom.simulate import run_qec_cycle
import numpy as np

# 1. Define the code
code = SteaneCode()

# 2. Define physical noise (i.i.d. depolarizing)
p = 0.01
single_qubit_depol = DepolarizingChannel(p=p)
noise_model = single_qubit_depol.iid(num_qubits=code.n_qubits)

# 3. Simulate many QEC cycles
shots = 200000
stats = run_qec_cycle(code=code, noise=noise_model, shots=shots)

# 4. Collect statistics
logical_X_rate = stats.logical_error_rate["X_logical"]
logical_Z_rate = stats.logical_error_rate["Z_logical"]
logical_Y_rate = stats.logical_error_rate["Y_logical"]

print("Logical X error rate:", logical_X_rate)
print("Logical Z error rate:", logical_Z_rate)
print("Logical Y error rate:", logical_Y_rate)

# Syndrome distribution
for syndrome, count in stats.syndrome_counts.items():
    print(syndrome, count / shots)

Mathematically, we expect (for small \(p\)):

  • Single weight-1 errors are typically perfectly corrected and do not cause logical errors.
  • Certain weight-2 errors will be mapped to nontrivial logical errors.

Thus the logical error rate is approximately \[ p_L \approx C \, p^2 \] for some constant \(C\) depending on the code structure and the error-correction strategy. Using el-loom simulations, we can estimate \(C\) numerically.

9.3 Syndrome Statistics and ML Decoding

Normalizing stats.syndrome_counts gives an empirical syndrome distribution \[ \hat{\Pr}[s] = \frac{\mathrm{count}(s)}{\mathrm{shots}}. \] For each syndrome \(s\), we can further resolve the logical error classes: \[ \hat{q}_{L|s} = \frac{\mathrm{count}(L,s)}{\mathrm{count}(s)}. \]

This information can be used to implement maximum-likelihood (ML) decoders or to train machine-learning-based decoders. el-loom provides functionality to output pairs of syndromes and logical errors directly or indirectly, enabling researchers to bridge “physical noise models” and “decoder behavior” with ease.

10. Advanced Topics: Channel Compression, Logical Channel Reconstruction, and Threshold Analysis

10.1 Logical Channel Reconstruction (Logical Channel Tomography)

Given an \([[n,k,d]]\) code and a physical noise channel \( \mathcal{N} \), we can (in principle) determine the full logical channel \[ \mathcal{N}_L: \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{2^k}) \to \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{2^k}), \] with Pauli representation \[ \mathcal{N}_L(\rho_L) = \sum_{L,L'} \chi_{L,L'} L\rho_L L'. \] For stabilizer codes under Pauli noise, \(\chi\) is typically diagonal: \[ \mathcal{N}_L(\rho_L) = \sum_{L} q_L L\rho_L L. \]

In practice, we estimate \(q_L\) using Monte Carlo methods. With el-loom, users can:

  • Sample errors \(E\) for each trial.
  • Determine the corresponding logical Pauli operator \(L(E)\).
  • Accumulate frequencies to obtain empirical estimates \( \hat{q}_L \).

10.2 Threshold Analysis and Scaling Behavior

In quantum error correction, a threshold is a critical physical error rate \(p_\mathrm{th}\) such that when \(p < p_\mathrm{th}\), the logical error rate can decrease exponentially with increasing code distance \(d\). Typically one expects \[ p_L(d, p) \sim \exp\left(-\alpha(p)\,d\right),\quad p<p_\mathrm{th}, \] and \( \alpha(p) \to 0 \) as \(p\to p_\mathrm{th}^- \).

In el-loom, we can systematically simulate a family of codes (for example, surface codes with distances \(d=3,5,7,\dots\)) and a range of \(p\) values: 

distances = [3,5,7,9]
ps = np.linspace(0.005, 0.02, 8)

data = []
for d in distances:
    code = SurfaceCode(distance=d)
    for p in ps:
        noise_model = DepolarizingChannel(p=p).iid(num_qubits=code.n_qubits)
        stats = run_qec_cycle(code=code, noise=noise_model, shots=50000)
        pL = stats.logical_error_rate["Z_logical"]  # for example
        data.append((d, p, pL))

We can then perform fits in Python to estimate the threshold and scaling exponents. Such statistical analyses are tightly linked to accurate physical noise modeling, and constitute one of the main application scenarios for which el-loom is designed.

11. Summary and Suggested Implementation Roadmap

In this lesson, we have rigorously derived from a mathematical standpoint:

  • The basic framework for combining quantum channels (especially Pauli channels) with stabilizer codes.
  • How to derive the syndrome distribution and logical channel \(\mathcal{N}_L\) from a physical noise channel \( \mathcal{N} \).
  • How to define noise models and stabilizer codes in el-loom, and how to simulate QEC cycles.

For further research and implementation, you may consider the following directions:

  1. Pauli twirling for non-Pauli noise and approximation error analysis: Compare the exact channel and its Pauli-twirled counterpart at the logical level, for example via diamond distance or differences in logical error rates.
  2. Impact of spatio-temporally correlated noise on thresholds: Construct correlated Pauli models, implement them in el-loom, and perform threshold analyses.
  3. Integration with specific hardware platforms: For example, translate the noise characteristics of superconducting qubits or trapped ions (such as amplitude damping + dephasing + leakage) into effective Pauli channel models, then use el-loom for code design and parameter optimization.
  4. Data-driven noise learning: Learn channels from experimental tomography data, and then apply the learned noise models in el-loom to evaluate QEC performance.

Once these techniques are mastered, readers can use el-loom as a complete platform for both experimental and theoretical studies “from physical noise to stabilizer-code performance”, enabling highly reproducible and scalable workflows for quantum error-correction simulations.
